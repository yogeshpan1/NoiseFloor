2015 Nepal Earthquake

  • What happened: A massive 7.8 magnitude earthquake struck Nepal on April 25, 2015.
  • Consequences: Devastating loss of life, widespread destruction of infrastructure, and a massive humanitarian crisis.
  • How it affected Nepal: Millions were displaced, triggering an urgent need for international aid, search and rescue, and rebuilding efforts.
  • How India Covered It (Media Bias): Indian media heavily covered their government's "Operation Maitri" rescue efforts. However, this coverage was widely criticized inside Nepal as being overly aggressive, PR-driven, and insensitive to victims, eventually sparking the #GoHomeIndianMedia trend.
  • How China Covered It: Chinese media focused on their quiet, structural aid and infrastructure rebuilding efforts, portraying themselves as a reliable, non-intrusive neighbor.

2015 India-Nepal Border Blockade

  • What happened: A 5-month economic and border blockade starting in September 2015, following the promulgation of Nepal's new constitution.
  • Consequences: Severe disruptions to trade, transport, and daily life across the country.
  • How it affected Nepal: Extreme shortages of essential supplies, including fuel, cooking gas, and vital medicines, causing a secondary humanitarian crisis shortly after the earthquake.
  • How India Covered It (Media Bias): Indian media heavily downplayed the blockade, portraying it entirely as an internal Nepali political protest by Madhesi groups. They accused Nepal of ignoring minority rights, effectively deflecting blame away from the Indian government.
  • How China Covered It: Chinese media actively criticized the blockade as "Indian hegemony" and bullying. China used this opportunity to step in and supply fuel to Nepal, breaking India's long-standing monopoly and portraying themselves as Nepal's savior.

How to read the Density Graph:

These two "bell curves" (bubbles) show the distribution of all news articles. The peak of the curve is the average tone. Because the red curve (India) is further to the left (negative numbers), it means Indian news was generally much more negative than Chinese news (cyan curve, closer to 0). When you click RUN TEST, the curves shift to show the exact data for the crisis you selected!

What are the "Metrics"?
  • Tone Divergence: This measures how positive or negative the news is (the mood of the coverage).
  • Event Frequency: This measures how much news is being published (the total volume of articles).
How to read the Statistical Matrix (HX-001 to HX-004):

A Null Hypothesis (H₀) is a default assumption. A P-Value under 0.05 means the assumption is WRONG (Rejected). Here's what we tested:

  • HX-001 [Tone]: Assumption: India and China have the same average tone. REJECTED. (Fact: India is much more negative).
  • HX-002 [Stability]: Assumption: India's tone changed between 2015 and 2025. STABLE. (Fact: India's tone has stayed exactly the same over 10 years).
  • HX-003 [Correlation]: Assumption: Indian and Chinese media do NOT copy each other. CONFIRMED. (Fact: When Indian news panics, Chinese news doesn't react. They cover different things).
  • HX-004 [Secular Regression]: Assumption: China's overall tone is not changing. REJECTED. (Fact: China is slowly drifting towards being slightly more critical over time).

What does this all mean in plain English?

  • Overall, China's news about Nepal is consistently much more positive and friendly than India's news, which tends to be more critical.
  • Over the last 10 years, India's tone has stayed exactly the same. However, China is slowly becoming slightly more critical of Nepal recently.
  • Day to day, India and China don't copy each other. When Indian news is negative, Chinese news isn't necessarily reacting to it. They cover entirely different stories about Nepal.

Methodology (How it works)

A beginner-friendly guide to how we collect data and calculate the results.

functions 1. How We Measure Tone

We use a simple statistical test to compare the "average mood" of news articles from India versus news articles from China to see if one is consistently more negative than the other.

We collect thousands of news articles.
Each article is rated from -100 (very negative) to +100 (very positive).
We average them out to find the overall tone.
The Default Assumption

India and China talk about Nepal in the exact same way. (We prove this wrong).

What We Actually Found

There is a massive, permanent difference in how the two countries report on Nepal.

tune 2. How We Score the News

We use a system called GDELT, which reads almost every news article published on the internet and assigns it a "Tone" score from -100 to +100.

01
Collect the Data
We download the raw data for all news articles worldwide.
02
Filter for Nepal
We throw away everything except articles where India or China are talking about Nepal.
03
Calculate the Average
We group the articles by day, and calculate the average score to get a single daily number.
